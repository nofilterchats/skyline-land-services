How Compensation Is Actually Calculated

"Fair market value" sounds precise, like it comes out of a formula everyone agrees on. In practice it's an estimate built from several moving parts — and most landowners only find out what those parts are after they've already gotten an offer.

It starts with fair market value — but that's not the whole formula

Fair market value is the baseline: what a willing buyer would pay a willing seller for that specific piece of land, under no pressure to act. It's a reasonable starting point, but it's rarely the final number. It gets adjusted based on exactly what's being taken, how it's being used, and what it does to the rest of your property.

Severance damage: the number most landowners don't know to ask about

If the taking affects the value of the land you keep — not just the strip that's being acquired — that's called severance damage, and it's often owed on top of the value of the parcel itself. A pipeline easement that splits a usable field, or cuts off access to part of your property, can reduce what the remainder is worth. This gets missed constantly, mostly because nobody brings it up unless you ask.

Comparables carry more weight than any single formula

What similar landowners nearby were actually paid for similar easements or takings is often the most persuasive evidence in a negotiation — more persuasive than an appraiser's methodology on its own. If a number seems low, comparable data is usually the fastest way to find out whether it actually is.

You're entitled to get your own appraisal

The appraisal behind the first offer was ordered by the party making the offer. You're allowed to get an independent one, and it's a normal part of the process, not a hostile move. Two appraisals landing far apart is common, and it's usually where real negotiation starts.
